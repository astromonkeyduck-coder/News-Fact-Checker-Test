# 7 Documents, 7 Lenses: The Geo-SPRITE DBQ Method

The Document-Based Question asks you to construct an argument using seven documents and your own knowledge. Geo-SPRITE gives you a reliable system for grouping those documents, sourcing them, and building the kind of complexity that earns top scores. This toolkit walks through every step: identifying the prompt's main lens, grouping documents into meaningful clusters, analyzing fourteen common document types, finding outside evidence, and layering in complexity.

---

## How to Identify the Prompt's Main Lens

Every DBQ prompt privileges one or two Geo-SPRITE categories, even when it doesn't name them explicitly. Your first job is to diagnose which lens the prompt is really about.

**Step 1: Underline the task verb and the topic noun.** A prompt that says "Evaluate the extent to which the Protestant Reformation changed political authority in Europe" is asking about change over time, and the topic noun is political authority. That tells you the primary lens is **Political**.

**Step 2: Identify the secondary lens hiding in the prompt.** The Reformation is a religious movement, so the prompt is really asking how **Religious** change drove **Political** change. You now have your two-lens frame before you read a single document.

**Step 3: Check for geographic or chronological constraints.** If the prompt says "in Western Europe between 1450 and 1648," that geographic boundary suggests you should think about how **Geography** shaped the phenomenon. Nations closer to Rome may have responded differently than those on the periphery.

**Step 4: Translate the prompt into a Geo-SPRITE sentence.** Before you touch the documents, write one sentence: "This prompt is primarily about [Lens A] and how [Lens B] influenced it." For the Reformation example: "This prompt is primarily about Political authority and how Religious upheaval reshaped it." That sentence becomes your thesis seed.

**Common prompt-to-lens translations:**

| Prompt language | Primary lens | Likely secondary lens |
|---|---|---|
| "political authority," "state power," "sovereignty" | Political | Social, Religious |
| "economic development," "trade," "wealth" | Economic | Technology, Geographic |
| "social structures," "class," "gender roles" | Social | Economic, Political |
| "religious reform," "church authority," "faith" | Religious | Political, Intellectual |
| "intellectual movements," "ideas," "philosophy" | Intellectual | Social, Political |
| "technological change," "innovation," "industry" | Technology | Economic, Social |
| "expansion," "colonialism," "migration" | Geographic/Imperialism | Economic, Political |

## How to Group Documents by Geo-SPRITE

You need at least two groups (three is better) and you must use at least four of the seven documents. Geo-SPRITE gives you a natural grouping system: each group is defined by a lens, and every document that reflects that lens goes into the group.

**The golden rule: a document can appear in more than one group.** A papal bull condemning Luther is both Religious (it defends Catholic doctrine) and Political (it asserts papal authority over secular rulers). Use that flexibility.

### 10 Grouping Templates

---

**Template 1: Political + Religious**

*When to use:* Prompts about church-state conflict, religious wars, toleration edicts, or theocratic governance.

*Example topics:* The Wars of Religion in France, the English Reformation, the Investiture Controversy's legacy, the Peace of Augsburg, the Edict of Nantes and its revocation.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 3, and 5 reveal that political rulers used religious reform as a tool to consolidate state power, while Documents 2 and 6 show that religious authorities resisted this encroachment to preserve ecclesiastical independence."

*Sourcing move:* Identify whether the author is a secular ruler or a church official. Their position shapes whether they frame religious change as liberation or as heresy.

*Outside evidence:* Henry VIII's Act of Supremacy (1534) merged crown and church authority. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established cuius regio, eius religio at the international level.

*Complexity move:* Acknowledge that the political-religious divide was not clean. Some rulers genuinely believed in reform (Frederick the Wise protecting Luther), while some clergy supported state power for pragmatic reasons (Gallicanism in France).

---

**Template 2: Economic + Social**

*When to use:* Prompts about industrialization, urbanization, labor movements, class conflict, or commercial revolutions.

*Example topics:* The effects of the Commercial Revolution, the social impact of the Industrial Revolution, the rise of the bourgeoisie, enclosure movements, the Atlantic slave trade's impact on European societies.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 2, 4, and 7 demonstrate that economic transformation created new wealth for merchant and industrial classes, while Documents 1, 3, and 5 illustrate how these same changes displaced traditional laborers and deepened social stratification."

*Sourcing move:* Check whether the author is a factory owner, a government inspector, a laborer, or a reformer. Each has a material interest in how economic change is portrayed.

*Outside evidence:* Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) justified free-market capitalism. Friedrich Engels documented Manchester working conditions in The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845).

*Complexity move:* Note that economic growth and social harm were not simply opposites. Some workers benefited from urbanization through higher wages and mobility, complicating any narrative of pure exploitation.

---

**Template 3: Intellectual + Political**

*When to use:* Prompts about Enlightenment influence on governance, revolutionary ideology, constitutionalism, or the relationship between philosophy and policy.

*Example topics:* Enlightenment influence on the French Revolution, the spread of liberal and nationalist ideas after 1815, the impact of Marxism on European politics, the intellectual origins of totalitarianism.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 4, and 6 show that Enlightenment thinkers provided the intellectual framework for challenging absolutism, while Documents 2, 5, and 7 reveal how political leaders selectively adopted or suppressed these ideas to serve state interests."

*Sourcing move:* Determine whether the author is a philosopher writing for an educated audience or a politician adapting ideas for a mass public. The gap between theory and implementation is where analysis lives.

*Outside evidence:* Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) influenced the separation of powers. The Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) translated Enlightenment theory into revolutionary law.

*Complexity move:* Argue that Enlightenment ideas could justify both democratic reform and authoritarian modernization (e.g., enlightened despotism under Frederick the Great or Catherine the Great).

---

**Template 4: Technology + Economic**

*When to use:* Prompts about industrialization, agricultural revolutions, transportation networks, military technology, or the printing press.

*Example topics:* The printing press and the Reformation, the impact of the railroad on European economies, the second Industrial Revolution, military technology in World War I, the Green Revolution.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 3, and 5 illustrate how technological innovations drove economic growth by increasing productivity and creating new markets, while Documents 2, 6, and 7 show that economic incentives and capital investment were prerequisites for technological development."

*Sourcing move:* Consider whether the source is a patent filing, a government commission report, a manufacturer's advertisement, or a worker's testimony. Each frames technology's impact differently.

*Outside evidence:* The spinning jenny and water frame transformed the British textile industry in the 1760s-1780s. The Bessemer process (1856) made steel production economically viable and fueled railroad expansion.

*Complexity move:* Challenge the idea that technology automatically produced economic benefit. The Luddite movement and continental Europe's slower adoption of British industrial technology show that context, policy, and social structures mediated technological impact.

---

**Template 5: Geography + Imperialism**

*When to use:* Prompts about colonial expansion, exploration, resource extraction, migration, or geopolitical competition.

*Example topics:* The Age of Exploration, the Scramble for Africa, the impact of geography on European political development, maritime empires versus land empires, decolonization.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 2, and 4 demonstrate that geographic advantages such as access to navigable rivers and Atlantic coastlines enabled certain European states to build colonial empires, while Documents 3, 6, and 7 reveal how imperial expansion reshaped the geography of trade, migration, and political boundaries."

*Sourcing move:* Maps and travel accounts are especially important here. Ask who made the map and for what purpose. A colonial administrator's map reflects imperial claims, not indigenous realities.

*Outside evidence:* Portugal's location on the Atlantic coast facilitated its early exploration of the West African coast under Prince Henry the Navigator. The Berlin Conference (1884-1885) partitioned Africa with little regard for existing geographic or ethnic boundaries.

*Complexity move:* Note that geography was a necessary but not sufficient condition for empire. The Ottoman Empire controlled strategic geography (the Bosporus, Eastern Mediterranean) but faced different imperial trajectories than Atlantic powers.

---

**Template 6: Social + Gender/Class**

*When to use:* Prompts about changing social hierarchies, women's roles, family structures, demographic change, or revolutionary social movements.

*Example topics:* Women's roles during the French Revolution, the impact of World War I on gender norms, the rise of the middle class, peasant revolts, the demographic transition.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 3, and 6 reveal how social upheaval temporarily opened new roles for women and lower classes, while Documents 2, 4, and 7 show that traditional gender and class hierarchies reasserted themselves once the crisis passed."

*Sourcing move:* Pay attention to the author's gender and class. A bourgeois woman writing about factory conditions has a different perspective than a female factory worker. Both are valuable, but for different reasons.

*Outside evidence:* Olympe de Gouges' Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) challenged the exclusion of women from revolutionary citizenship. The Napoleonic Code (1804) rolled back women's legal gains from the Revolution.

*Complexity move:* Argue that class and gender did not always align. Upper-class women often had more agency than lower-class men in certain contexts, complicating any simple hierarchy of oppression.

---

**Template 7: Political + Diplomatic**

*When to use:* Prompts about alliances, treaties, balance of power, wars, or international relations.

*Example topics:* The Congress of Vienna, the alliance system before World War I, the Treaty of Versailles, the Cold War division of Europe, European integration.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 4, and 5 show that domestic political pressures shaped diplomatic positions, while Documents 2, 3, and 7 reveal how international agreements constrained or redirected internal political developments."

*Sourcing move:* Diplomatic documents are written for multiple audiences: the foreign counterpart, the domestic public, and posterity. Identify which audience the author is primarily addressing.

*Outside evidence:* Metternich's Concert of Europe sought to suppress liberal nationalism after 1815. Bismarck's alliance system (Triple Alliance, Reinsurance Treaty) aimed to isolate France while maintaining German security.

*Complexity move:* Show that the domestic/international boundary was porous. Bismarck used foreign policy crises to unify Germany internally, blurring the line between diplomacy and domestic politics.

---

**Template 8: Religious + Social**

*When to use:* Prompts about the social role of the church, popular piety, religious minorities, secularization, or religiously motivated social reform.

*Example topics:* The role of the Catholic Church in medieval society, the social impact of the Reformation on family and community, Jewish emancipation, the de-Christianization campaign during the French Revolution, Christian socialism.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 2, 3, and 5 demonstrate that religious institutions shaped social norms around marriage, education, and charity, while Documents 1, 6, and 7 show that social changes such as urbanization and literacy undermined traditional religious authority."

*Sourcing move:* Religious texts written by clergy reflect institutional priorities. Lay religious writings (diaries, letters) reveal how ordinary people experienced faith in daily social life.

*Outside evidence:* The Council of Trent (1545-1563) reasserted Catholic social teaching on marriage and education. John Wesley's Methodist movement (18th century) provided social community and moral structure for the English working class.

*Complexity move:* Argue that secularization was uneven. France experienced aggressive de-Christianization during the Revolution, while religious practice remained central to social life in rural Southern and Eastern Europe well into the twentieth century.

---

**Template 9: Intellectual + Religious**

*When to use:* Prompts about the Scientific Revolution, the relationship between faith and reason, humanism, the Enlightenment critique of religion, or theological debates.

*Example topics:* The conflict between Galileo and the Church, the Enlightenment critique of organized religion, Erasmus versus Luther on free will, the impact of Darwin on European religious thought, Renaissance humanism.

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 3, and 4 illustrate how new intellectual methods challenged religious orthodoxy by prioritizing empirical observation and reason, while Documents 2, 5, and 7 show that religious thinkers adapted to and sometimes incorporated new intellectual frameworks."

*Sourcing move:* Determine whether the author is a scientist writing cautiously to avoid censorship, a theologian defending doctrine, or a philosophe deliberately provoking. The rhetorical context shapes the argument.

*Outside evidence:* Copernicus dedicated De Revolutionibus (1543) to the Pope, attempting to frame heliocentrism as compatible with faith. Voltaire's attacks on the Catholic Church ("Ecrasez l'infame") represented the sharpest Enlightenment rejection of religious authority.

*Complexity move:* Resist the "science versus religion" narrative. Newton was deeply religious. Many Jesuits contributed to scientific advancement. The relationship between intellectual change and religious belief was collaborative as often as it was adversarial.

---

**Template 10: Economic + Political**

*When to use:* Prompts about mercantilism, taxation, revolution, welfare states, economic policy, or the relationship between wealth and power.

*Example topics:* Mercantilism and state-building, the economic causes of the French Revolution, the rise of the welfare state, the economic dimensions of fascism, European economic integration (EEC/EU).

*Sample grouping sentence:* "Documents 1, 2, and 6 show that states used economic policy to consolidate political power, while Documents 3, 5, and 7 reveal that economic grievances fueled political opposition and revolution."

*Sourcing move:* Government economic reports reflect state priorities. Petitions and pamphlets from merchants or peasants reveal how economic policy was experienced at the ground level.

*Outside evidence:* Colbert's mercantilist policies under Louis XIV used tariffs and state-sponsored manufacturing to fund absolutist ambitions. The cahiers de doleances (1789) documented widespread economic grievances that fueled the French Revolution.

*Complexity move:* Show that economic and political power did not always move in the same direction. The Dutch Republic was an economic powerhouse with a decentralized political system, while Spain's centralized monarchy presided over economic decline in the seventeenth century.

## Document Analysis by Type

Every AP Euro DBQ draws from a limited set of document types. Knowing what each type typically reveals through a Geo-SPRITE lens saves you time and sharpens your analysis.

### 1. Law or Decree

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political
**What to look for:** Who issued it, who is being regulated, what behavior is being mandated or prohibited. Laws reveal state priorities and the limits of state power.
**Sourcing tip:** A law's existence does not mean it was enforced. The gap between legislation and implementation is a productive analytical space.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* The Napoleonic Code (1804) was issued during the consolidation of Napoleonic power. *Intended audience:* All French citizens and administrators in conquered territories. *Purpose:* To standardize legal practice and embed Enlightenment principles of property and contract into law while also restricting women's legal autonomy. *Point of view:* Reflects Napoleon's desire to project rational governance while maintaining patriarchal social order.

### 2. Treaty

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political/Diplomatic
**What to look for:** Who the signatories are, what territory or rights are exchanged, what enforcement mechanisms exist, and who is excluded from the agreement.
**Sourcing tip:** Treaties represent negotiated outcomes, not unilateral statements. What each side conceded tells you about their relative power.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* The Treaty of Westphalia (1648) ended the Thirty Years' War. *Intended audience:* European sovereigns and the diplomatic community. *Purpose:* To establish a new framework for European state sovereignty and religious coexistence. *Point of view:* Favored territorial princes over imperial centralization and marginalized papal authority in international affairs.

### 3. Political Speech

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political
**What to look for:** The speaker's office, the audience, the rhetorical appeals (fear, pride, justice), and what action the speech calls for.
**Sourcing tip:** Speeches are performative. The speaker is trying to move an audience, not describe reality objectively. Identify what response the speaker wants.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* Churchill's "Iron Curtain" speech (1946) came amid growing Soviet-Western tensions. *Intended audience:* The American public and policymakers. *Purpose:* To mobilize Anglo-American solidarity against Soviet expansion. *Point of view:* Churchill, no longer Prime Minister, sought to influence the emerging Cold War alignment from outside formal office.

### 4. Religious Text

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Religious
**What to look for:** Doctrinal claims, moral prescriptions, references to authority (scripture, tradition, papal decree), and the relationship between spiritual and temporal power.
**Sourcing tip:** Religious texts often serve political or social purposes beyond theology. Luther's Ninety-Five Theses were theological, but their social and political implications were enormous.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536) systematized Reformed theology during the early Reformation. *Intended audience:* Educated clergy and literate laity. *Purpose:* To provide a comprehensive doctrinal framework for Protestant Christianity. *Point of view:* Calvin's emphasis on predestination and moral discipline reflected his vision of a godly community governed by religious law.

### 5. Philosophical Text

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Intellectual
**What to look for:** The central argument, the method of reasoning (empirical, deductive, dialectical), the intellectual tradition being engaged, and the practical implications.
**Sourcing tip:** Philosophers write for educated elites. Their influence on policy or popular belief is often indirect and mediated by popularizers, journalists, and politicians.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* Locke's Second Treatise of Government (1689) was published after the Glorious Revolution. *Intended audience:* The English political class. *Purpose:* To justify constitutional government based on natural rights and the social contract. *Point of view:* Locke's theory of property rights reflected the interests of the English landed gentry who supported the Whig settlement.

### 6. Economic Statistics

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Economic
**What to look for:** What is being measured, over what time period, who collected the data, and what trends are visible.
**Sourcing tip:** Statistics are never neutral. Who collected them, what they chose to measure, and what they omitted are all analytical opportunities.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* British industrial output statistics from the 1840s reflect the height of the first Industrial Revolution. *Intended audience:* Parliament and government administrators. *Purpose:* To demonstrate economic growth and justify free-trade policy. *Point of view:* Government statistics emphasized aggregate growth, potentially masking regional variation and working-class hardship.

### 7. Map

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Geographic/Political
**What to look for:** What territory is shown, whose perspective it represents, what boundaries or routes are emphasized, and what is left off the map.
**Sourcing tip:** Maps are arguments. A colonial map claims territory. A demographic map highlights migration. Ask what the mapmaker wants the viewer to believe about the space.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* A map of European colonial possessions in Africa circa 1914 reflects the post-Berlin Conference partition. *Intended audience:* European policymakers, educators, and the general public. *Purpose:* To visualize and legitimize imperial claims. *Point of view:* The map erases African political organization and presents European control as complete and orderly.

### 8. Image or Political Cartoon

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political or Social
**What to look for:** Symbols, caricature, exaggeration, labeling, the target of satire, and the intended emotional response.
**Sourcing tip:** Cartoons are designed to persuade through humor or outrage. Identify the cartoonist's political alignment and the publication's audience.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* A Punch cartoon from the 1880s depicting Bismarck as a juggler managing European alliances. *Intended audience:* The British educated public. *Purpose:* To comment on the complexity and fragility of Bismarck's diplomatic system. *Point of view:* British ambivalence toward continental power politics, reflecting a preference for splendid isolation.

### 9. Painting

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Social, Intellectual, or Religious
**What to look for:** Subject matter, composition, patronage, artistic style, and the cultural values being expressed or idealized.
**Sourcing tip:** Commissioned paintings serve the patron's interests. A royal portrait flatters. A history painting glorifies. Identify the patron and the occasion.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* David's The Coronation of Napoleon (1807) was commissioned by Napoleon himself. *Intended audience:* The French public and European courts. *Purpose:* To legitimize Napoleon's authority by depicting him crowning himself, independent of papal sanction. *Point of view:* David, a committed revolutionary turned Napoleonic propagandist, presented Napoleon as the embodiment of post-revolutionary order.

### 10. Letter or Diary

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Social (personal experience) or Political (if from an official)
**What to look for:** The relationship between writer and recipient, the emotional tone, what the writer reveals unintentionally, and whether the letter was intended for a wider audience.
**Sourcing tip:** Private letters are among the most candid sources, but "private" is relative. Many political figures wrote letters knowing they would be published posthumously.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* A letter from a German soldier at Verdun (1916) describes trench conditions. *Intended audience:* The soldier's family. *Purpose:* To maintain personal connection while processing traumatic experience. *Point of view:* The soldier's account is shaped by censorship, the desire not to alarm loved ones, and the immediate physical reality of the Western Front.

### 11. Memoir

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Social or Political
**What to look for:** The gap between the events described and the time of writing, the author's self-presentation, what is emphasized or omitted, and the political context of publication.
**Sourcing tip:** Memoirs are retrospective constructions. The author knows how the story ended and shapes the narrative accordingly.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* Metternich's memoirs, published posthumously, cover his role at the Congress of Vienna and the conservative restoration. *Intended audience:* Posterity and the diplomatic community. *Purpose:* To justify his conservative policies and defend the Concert of Europe. *Point of view:* Metternich presents himself as the architect of stability, downplaying the repressive dimensions of the Carlsbad Decrees and censorship systems.

### 12. Newspaper Article

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political or Social
**What to look for:** The publication's political orientation, the framing of events, what sources are cited, and whether the tone is reportorial, editorial, or propagandistic.
**Sourcing tip:** Newspapers serve their readership's interests and ideology. A conservative paper and a socialist paper cover the same strike very differently.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* A Times of London article reporting on the Peterloo Massacre (1819). *Intended audience:* The British middle and upper classes. *Purpose:* To inform readers about the government's suppression of a reform rally. *Point of view:* Depending on the reporter's sympathies, the article may frame the crowd as a dangerous mob or as peaceful citizens exercising their rights.

### 13. Propaganda Poster

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political or Social
**What to look for:** Visual rhetoric, emotional appeals, the enemy being constructed, the behavior being promoted, and the sponsoring authority.
**Sourcing tip:** Propaganda posters are state-directed messaging. They reveal not what people believe, but what the state wants them to believe. The gap between the message and reality is analytically rich.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* A Soviet poster from the 1930s depicting collective farming as joyful and abundant. *Intended audience:* Soviet workers and peasants. *Purpose:* To generate enthusiasm for collectivization during a period of famine and coercion. *Point of view:* The state's idealized vision directly contradicts the reality of the Holodomor and widespread resistance to collectivization.

### 14. Government Report

**Most likely Geo-SPRITE lens:** Political or Economic
**What to look for:** What problem the report was commissioned to investigate, what data it presents, what it recommends, and whether the recommendations were implemented.
**Sourcing tip:** Government reports are produced within bureaucratic and political constraints. A report on factory conditions may be honest, but its recommendations are limited by what the government is willing to enforce.
**Sample HIPP:** *Historical context:* The Sadler Committee Report (1832) investigated child labor in British factories. *Intended audience:* Parliament. *Purpose:* To build the case for labor regulation by documenting abuses. *Point of view:* The committee was sympathetic to reform, and its questioning style elicited the most dramatic testimonies. Critics argued the report was biased toward emotional impact over representativeness.

## How to Find Outside Evidence by Geo-SPRITE

Outside evidence is specific historical information not found in the documents. The rubric requires at least one piece of outside evidence used to support your argument. Geo-SPRITE gives you a systematic way to generate it.

**Method:** After grouping your documents, identify the Geo-SPRITE lens of each group. Then ask: "What do I know about this lens in this time period that is not in the documents?"

| Lens | Question to ask yourself | Example outside evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Geographic | What geographic factors shaped this development? | Russia's lack of warm-water ports drove its expansionist foreign policy |
| Social | What social groups were affected or mobilized? | The sans-culottes drove radical phases of the French Revolution |
| Political | What state actions or political movements were relevant? | The Carlsbad Decrees (1819) suppressed liberal nationalist movements |
| Religious | What religious movements or conflicts were relevant? | The Jesuits led the Counter-Reformation's educational and missionary efforts |
| Intellectual | What ideas or thinkers were influential? | Rousseau's concept of the general will influenced revolutionary ideology |
| Technology | What inventions or technical changes mattered? | The railroad enabled rapid troop mobilization in the Franco-Prussian War |
| Economic | What economic systems or changes were at play? | The price revolution of the sixteenth century destabilized fixed-income nobility |

**Warning:** Outside evidence must be specific. "The economy was bad" is not outside evidence. "The Continental System (1806) disrupted British trade with Europe and provoked smuggling networks" is.

## How to Build Complexity by Adding a Second Lens

The complexity point is the hardest point on the rubric. The most reliable way to earn it is to demonstrate that the historical development cannot be explained by a single Geo-SPRITE lens alone.

**Strategy 1: Qualify your argument with a second lens.** If your thesis argues that political factors drove the French Revolution, your complexity paragraph can argue that economic factors (debt crisis, bread prices) created the preconditions that made political revolution possible. This shows that political factors were necessary but not sufficient.

**Strategy 2: Show how lenses interacted.** Argue that the Protestant Reformation was not simply a Religious event or a Political event, but that religious conviction and political opportunism reinforced each other. Princes who adopted Lutheranism gained both theological justification and the material wealth of confiscated church properties.

**Strategy 3: Analyze an alternative perspective through a different lens.** If most of your documents support an Intellectual interpretation of the Scientific Revolution, your complexity move can explore how Social factors (patronage networks, the culture of gentlemanly science, gender exclusion) shaped which ideas gained traction and which were marginalized.

**Strategy 4: Connect to a different time period or region using a new lens.** If your argument focuses on Economic nationalism in the nineteenth century, your complexity move can connect to how Geographic factors (resource distribution, access to coal and iron) determined which nations could industrialize and which remained agricultural peripheries.

## Category Dumping Warning

Category dumping is the most common Geo-SPRITE mistake. It happens when a student lists all seven categories in their thesis or opening paragraph without connecting them to the actual argument.

**What it looks like:** "The French Revolution was caused by social, political, religious, intellectual, technological, economic, and geographic factors." This sentence says nothing. It applies to every event in history.

**Why it fails:** The rubric rewards argumentation, not categorization. Listing categories is not analysis. Grouping documents by category is a tool for building an argument, not a substitute for one.

**How to avoid it:**

1. Choose two or three lenses maximum for your thesis.
2. Make sure each lens does analytical work. "Political factors drove the revolution" is a claim. "Social, political, religious, intellectual, technological, economic, and geographic factors drove the revolution" is a list.
3. Use the remaining lenses for complexity, outside evidence, or sourcing, not for your thesis.
4. If you find yourself writing "SPRITE" as an acronym in your essay, stop. The reader should see your argument, not your study method.

The goal is not to mention every category. The goal is to use the categories that best explain the historical development, deploy them as analytical tools, and demonstrate that you understand how multiple forces interacted to produce complex outcomes.
