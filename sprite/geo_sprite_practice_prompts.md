## How to Use This Document

Each prompt is organized by reasoning process (Causation, Comparison, CCOT, DBQ-style). For every prompt you will find:

- The prompt text (labeled PRACTICE PROMPT)
- Best thesis direction
- Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses
- Best 3 evidence items
- Best context starter

Prompts are distributed across all 9 AP European History units so that no period is neglected during review.

## Causation Prompts

### Causation 1 (Unit 1)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which geographic factors caused the shift in economic power from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic world in the period 1450-1600.

- **Best thesis direction:** Geographic factors were a necessary but insufficient cause; the shift required the convergence of navigational technology, state competition on the Iberian Peninsula, and the geographic reality that Atlantic-facing states had direct access to wind and current systems linking them to the Americas and the African coast.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Geography, Technology, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Portugal's position on the Atlantic and Prince Henry's navigation school at Sagres; (2) Spain's completion of the Reconquista in 1492 freeing resources for Atlantic exploration; (3) the development of the caravel and lateen sail enabling navigation of Atlantic wind patterns.
- **Best context starter:** For centuries the Mediterranean had been the center of European commerce, with Italian city-states like Venice and Genoa dominating trade routes to Asia through middlemen in the Ottoman Empire.
- **Complexity move:** Qualify the argument by noting that geographic factors alone cannot explain the timing -- Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453) simultaneously pushed and pulled commerce westward, showing that political disruption in the East was a co-cause alongside Atlantic geography.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Simply listing explorers and their voyages without explaining the causal mechanism connecting geography to economic power shifts.

### Causation 2 (Unit 2)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the printing press caused the success of the Protestant Reformation in the period 1517-1555.

- **Best thesis direction:** The printing press was a critical accelerant that transformed what could have been a local theological dispute into a continent-wide movement, but its impact depended on pre-existing social grievances against the Church and political incentives for German princes to break with Rome.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Technology, Intellectual, Political
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Luther's 95 Theses were reprinted and distributed across Germany within weeks of their posting in 1517; (2) vernacular pamphlets and woodcuts reached literate and semi-literate audiences far beyond university towns; (3) German princes like Frederick the Wise used print culture to publicize their protection of Luther, linking religious reform to political independence from the Emperor.
- **Best context starter:** Before 1450, the dissemination of ideas in Europe depended on hand-copied manuscripts, limiting the speed and reach of intellectual movements; earlier reform efforts by Wycliffe and Hus had been largely contained within their regions.
- **Complexity move:** Compare the Reformation's print-driven spread in the Holy Roman Empire to the slower, more top-down adoption of Anglicanism in England, where the printing press mattered less than royal authority, demonstrating that technology's causal role varied by political context.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating the printing press as the sole cause and ignoring the social, economic, and political conditions that made audiences receptive to Luther's message.

### Causation 3 (Unit 3)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which religious conflict caused the development of new theories of political sovereignty in the period 1562-1651.

- **Best thesis direction:** The devastation of religious wars -- particularly the French Wars of Religion and the Thirty Years' War -- directly caused political thinkers to seek secular bases for state authority, producing theories of sovereignty (Bodin, Hobbes) that prioritized order and centralized power over confessional allegiance.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Religious, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Jean Bodin's Six Books of the Commonwealth (1576), written during the French Wars of Religion, argued for indivisible sovereign power; (2) The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the principle of state sovereignty over religious matters (cuius regio, eius religio extended); (3) Hobbes's Leviathan (1651), written during the English Civil War, grounded absolute authority in rational self-interest rather than divine right.
- **Best context starter:** The medieval political order had rested on a shared Christian framework in which the Pope and the Emperor exercised complementary spiritual and temporal authority over a theoretically unified Christendom.
- **Complexity move:** Note the ironic long-term consequence: theories of sovereignty developed to end religious conflict eventually enabled secular states to pursue power politics (raison d'etat), which itself became a new source of European conflict in the 17th and 18th centuries.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Listing religious wars without connecting them causally to specific intellectual developments in political theory.

### Causation 4 (Unit 4)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which commercial agriculture caused changes in European social structures in the period 1600-1750.

- **Best thesis direction:** The expansion of commercial agriculture, driven by global trade networks and enclosure movements, caused the erosion of traditional peasant communities in Western Europe by creating a landless labor class, while simultaneously reinforcing serfdom in Eastern Europe where nobles expanded grain production for export.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Social, Geography
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) English enclosure acts consolidated common lands into private holdings, displacing subsistence farmers; (2) The "second serfdom" in Prussia, Poland, and Russia intensified peasant obligations as noble landlords expanded grain exports to Western markets; (3) The Dutch agricultural revolution introduced crop rotation and land reclamation, creating a prosperous commercial farming class.
- **Best context starter:** Medieval European agriculture had been organized around the manorial system, in which peasants held customary rights to common lands in exchange for labor obligations to their lord.
- **Complexity move:** Contrast the divergent social effects across geography: commercial agriculture liberated some Western European peasants into wage labor (the proto-proletariat) while deepening Eastern European serfdom, showing that the same economic force produced opposite social outcomes depending on regional power structures.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing agricultural changes without specifying how they altered class relationships and social hierarchies.

### Causation 5 (Unit 5)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which Enlightenment ideas caused the French Revolution in 1789.

- **Best thesis direction:** Enlightenment ideas provided the ideological vocabulary and legitimacy for revolutionary action, but the Revolution's immediate causes were fiscal crisis, social resentment of aristocratic privilege, and the political failure of the monarchy to reform -- Enlightenment thought shaped the Revolution's direction more than it caused its outbreak.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Intellectual, Political, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Rousseau's Social Contract provided the concept of popular sovereignty that informed the Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen; (2) Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws influenced the National Assembly's attempts to create constitutional government; (3) the salons and reading clubs of Paris disseminated Enlightenment critiques of absolutism to the educated bourgeoisie who led the Third Estate.
- **Best context starter:** By the 1780s, France faced a convergence of crises: massive state debt from involvement in the American Revolution, a regressive tax system that exempted the nobility and clergy, and a series of harvest failures that raised bread prices for the urban and rural poor.
- **Complexity move:** Distinguish between Enlightenment ideas as long-term intellectual causes and the immediate triggers (calling of the Estates-General, storming of the Bastille) to show that ideas alone were insufficient without a political and economic crisis that made the old regime unable to maintain legitimacy.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Claiming the philosophes "caused" the Revolution directly, as if reading Voltaire made people revolt, without explaining the material conditions that made revolutionary ideas actionable.

### Causation 6 (Unit 6)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which industrialization caused urbanization in Britain in the period 1760-1850.

- **Best thesis direction:** Industrialization was the primary cause of rapid urbanization as factory production concentrated labor demand in specific locations near coal, iron, and transportation networks, but urbanization was also shaped by agricultural displacement (enclosures) and demographic growth that pushed rural populations toward cities.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Technology, Economic, Geography, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Manchester grew from roughly 25,000 people in 1772 to over 300,000 by 1850, driven by textile mills; (2) canal and railway construction (Bridgewater Canal, Liverpool-Manchester Railway) created transportation corridors that determined which towns industrialized; (3) the factory system required concentrated labor, replacing dispersed cottage industry with centralized production.
- **Best context starter:** Before industrialization, the majority of Britain's population lived in rural areas and engaged in agricultural labor or domestic manufacturing; London was the only city of significant size.
- **Complexity move:** Acknowledge that urbanization also caused further industrialization in a feedback loop -- concentrated populations created consumer markets, labor pools, and infrastructure that attracted more industry, making the causal relationship bidirectional rather than unidirectional.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing urban conditions (pollution, overcrowding, disease) without explaining the causal chain from industrialization to urban growth.

### Causation 7 (Unit 7)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which nationalism caused the outbreak of World War I in 1914.

- **Best thesis direction:** Nationalism was a necessary enabling cause that created the conditions for war -- particularly through pan-Slavism, Franco-German rivalry, and imperial competition -- but the war's immediate outbreak required the interaction of alliance systems, militarism, and a specific political crisis (the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand) that activated these nationalist tensions.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Geography
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Pan-Slavic nationalism fueled Serbian ambitions in Bosnia and drew Russia into the crisis as protector of Slavic peoples; (2) French revanchism over Alsace-Lorraine (lost in 1871) sustained Franco-German hostility; (3) nationalist movements within Austria-Hungary (Czech, South Slav, Hungarian) weakened the empire's internal cohesion and made it desperate to suppress Serbian nationalism externally.
- **Best context starter:** The century following the Congress of Vienna (1815) saw the transformation of European politics from a system managed by multinational empires to one increasingly defined by nation-states, as demonstrated by the unifications of Italy and Germany.
- **Complexity move:** Distinguish between civic nationalism (which could be accommodated within existing states) and ethnic nationalism (which was inherently destabilizing to multinational empires), arguing that the specific form of nationalism in the Balkans and Central Europe was more dangerous than nationalism per se.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Listing all the "MAIN" causes (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism) without analytically evaluating the relative weight of nationalism as a causal factor.

### Causation 8 (Unit 8)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the Treaty of Versailles caused the rise of totalitarian regimes in Europe in the period 1919-1939.

- **Best thesis direction:** Versailles created conditions that enabled totalitarianism -- economic devastation in Germany, border disputes across Eastern Europe, and a sense of national humiliation -- but the rise of specific regimes required additional causes including the Great Depression, weak democratic institutions, and the ideological appeal of fascism and communism.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Economic, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The war guilt clause (Article 231) and reparations payments fueled German resentment that Hitler exploited; (2) the creation of new nation-states with significant ethnic minorities (Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Poland) generated irredentist conflicts; (3) Italy's sense of a "mutilated victory" despite fighting on the Allied side contributed to Mussolini's nationalist appeal.
- **Best context starter:** World War I had destroyed four empires (Ottoman, Russian, Austro-Hungarian, German), leaving a power vacuum across Central and Eastern Europe and discrediting the old political order.
- **Complexity move:** Compare Germany and France: both suffered enormously in WWI, but France maintained its democratic republic while Germany's Weimar Republic collapsed, suggesting that Versailles was a contributing factor but not a sufficient cause -- domestic political culture and institutional strength mattered independently.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating Versailles as the sole cause of Hitler's rise without accounting for the Great Depression, the failures of the Weimar Republic, or the choices of German political elites.

### Causation 9 (Unit 9)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which economic integration caused the end of the Cold War division of Europe in the period 1975-1991.

- **Best thesis direction:** Economic integration in Western Europe (through the EEC/EU) demonstrated the superiority of market economies and created a magnetic pull on Eastern European populations, but the Cold War's end also required Soviet economic stagnation, Gorbachev's reforms, and popular mobilization in Eastern Europe.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Political, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The Helsinki Accords (1975) linked economic cooperation to human rights, giving dissidents a framework; (2) the visible prosperity gap between West and East Germany undermined communist legitimacy; (3) Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika attempted economic reform but inadvertently loosened political control.
- **Best context starter:** After 1945, Europe was divided into two competing economic systems: the market economies of Western Europe, increasingly integrated through the European Economic Community, and the command economies of the Soviet bloc, coordinated through COMECON.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that economic factors operated on different timescales -- long-term stagnation eroded communist legitimacy over decades, while short-term crises (oil price drops, Soviet Afghan war costs) created the immediate fiscal pressures that forced Gorbachev to reform.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Attributing the end of the Cold War solely to Reagan or Gorbachev as individuals without analyzing the structural economic forces at work.

## Comparison Prompts

### Comparison 1 (Units 1-2)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the methods used by the Spanish and Portuguese empires to establish colonial control in the period 1450-1600.

- **Best thesis direction:** Both empires used a combination of military conquest, religious mission, and commercial extraction, but Spain relied more heavily on direct territorial control and settlement (encomienda system), while Portugal established a network of coastal trading posts (feitorias) that prioritized commercial access over territorial domination, a difference explained largely by geography and the nature of the societies they encountered.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Geography, Economic, Religious
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Spain's encomienda system granted colonists control over indigenous labor in densely populated Mesoamerica and the Andes; (2) Portugal's feitoria system established fortified trading posts along the African and Asian coasts (Elmina, Goa, Malacca); (3) both empires used Catholic missionary activity as a tool of colonization (Jesuits in Brazil, Franciscans in New Spain).
- **Best context starter:** The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the non-Christian world between Spain and Portugal, reflecting papal authority and Iberian dominance of early Atlantic exploration.
- **Complexity move:** Note that the distinction between Spanish territorial control and Portuguese commercial networks blurred over time -- Portugal developed plantation agriculture in Brazil, while Spain maintained trading relationships in the Philippines -- showing that colonial methods adapted to local conditions regardless of initial imperial strategy.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Simply describing each empire separately without drawing direct, analytical comparisons of their methods and explaining reasons for similarities and differences.

### Comparison 2 (Unit 3)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the political systems of absolute monarchy in France under Louis XIV and constitutional monarchy in England after the Glorious Revolution (1688).

- **Best thesis direction:** Both systems concentrated effective power in a relatively narrow elite and used Protestant-Catholic tensions as political tools, but they differed fundamentally in where sovereignty was located: in France, sovereignty rested with the monarch who ruled by divine right and governed without a representative legislature, while in England, sovereignty was shared between the Crown and Parliament through the Bill of Rights (1689).
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Religious, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Louis XIV's revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) demonstrated absolute royal authority over religious policy; (2) the English Bill of Rights (1689) required parliamentary consent for taxation and prohibited a standing army without parliamentary approval; (3) Louis XIV's construction of Versailles centralized the nobility under royal surveillance, while English aristocrats maintained independent power bases through Parliament.
- **Best context starter:** The seventeenth century saw European states experimenting with different solutions to the problem of political order after a century of religious wars, with absolutism and constitutionalism emerging as the two dominant models.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the difference was less absolute than textbook categories suggest: Louis XIV still had to negotiate with provincial parlements and corporate bodies, while the English Parliament represented a narrow propertied elite, not the general population -- both systems were oligarchic in practice even if they differed in constitutional theory.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating absolutism and constitutionalism as simple binaries (tyranny vs. freedom) without acknowledging the complexity and limitations of both systems.

### Comparison 3 (Unit 4)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the economic policies of mercantilism as practiced by France under Colbert and the Dutch Republic in the seventeenth century.

- **Best thesis direction:** Both pursued national wealth accumulation and commercial dominance, but Colbert's mercantilism was state-directed (tariffs, subsidies, royal manufactures), while the Dutch model relied on private enterprise, financial innovation, and open markets coordinated through institutions like the Dutch East India Company and the Amsterdam Exchange Bank -- reflecting their different political structures (absolutism vs. decentralized republic).
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Political, Geography
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Colbert established royal manufactures (Gobelins tapestries) and imposed tariffs to reduce imports; (2) the Dutch East India Company (VOC), a joint-stock company, operated as a quasi-state entity that controlled Asian trade; (3) the Amsterdam Exchange Bank provided stable currency and credit that made Amsterdam the financial capital of Europe.
- **Best context starter:** The seventeenth century was an era of intense commercial rivalry among European states, in which national wealth was seen as finite and trade as a form of warfare conducted by other means.
- **Complexity move:** Note the geographic dimension: France's large land area and agricultural base made a state-directed industrial policy logical, while the Dutch Republic's small size, lack of natural resources, and dependence on maritime trade required financial openness and commercial flexibility -- geography shaped economic ideology.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Defining mercantilism as a single uniform policy and failing to distinguish between its different national implementations.

### Comparison 4 (Unit 5)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the causes and outcomes of the American Revolution (1776) and the French Revolution (1789).

- **Best thesis direction:** Both revolutions drew on Enlightenment principles of natural rights and popular sovereignty, but the American Revolution was primarily a colonial independence movement that preserved existing social hierarchies, while the French Revolution was a domestic social upheaval that attempted to fundamentally restructure class relations -- a difference rooted in the presence of an entrenched feudal aristocracy in France and its absence in America.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Intellectual, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Both produced founding documents influenced by Locke and Montesquieu (Declaration of Independence, Declaration of the Rights of Man); (2) the French Revolution abolished feudal privileges (August 4 Decrees, 1789) -- an issue that had no parallel in America; (3) the American Revolution produced stable constitutional government, while the French Revolution led to the Terror, military dictatorship, and decades of political instability.
- **Best context starter:** The late eighteenth century saw a wave of revolutionary movements across the Atlantic world, fueled by Enlightenment ideas about natural rights, the social contract, and the consent of the governed.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the revolutions differed not primarily in ideology but in the social structures they confronted: America lacked a feudal aristocracy and established church to overthrow, making a moderate political revolution sufficient, while France's deeply entrenched Old Regime required (or provoked) a more radical transformation.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Listing similarities and differences without a clear thesis about what explains the differences.

### Comparison 5 (Unit 6)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the effects of industrialization on the working class in Britain and Russia in the period 1800-1914.

- **Best thesis direction:** Industrialization created an urban working class in both countries that experienced harsh labor conditions, but the political context differed dramatically: Britain's parliamentary system allowed gradual reform (Factory Acts, trade unions, Chartism), while Russia's autocracy suppressed labor organization and channeled working-class grievances toward revolutionary movements.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Social, Political, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) British Factory Acts (1833, 1847) incrementally regulated child labor and working hours; (2) Russian industrialization, concentrated in the 1890s under Witte's policies, created a compressed proletarianization without accompanying political liberalization; (3) the British trade union movement gained legal recognition (1871) while Russian unions were suppressed, contributing to the appeal of Marxist revolutionary parties.
- **Best context starter:** The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the late eighteenth century and spread unevenly across Europe, reaching Russia significantly later and under fundamentally different political conditions.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the timing of industrialization relative to political development was the key variable: Britain industrialized gradually alongside expanding political participation, while Russia's rapid, state-directed industrialization under autocracy created a volatile combination of economic modernization and political repression.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing conditions in each country separately without making direct comparative analytical points.

### Comparison 6 (Unit 7)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare Italian Fascism under Mussolini and German Nazism under Hitler as political systems in the interwar period.

- **Best thesis direction:** Both were authoritarian, nationalist, anti-communist movements that used mass mobilization and propaganda, but Nazism was more ideologically radical in its biological racism and pursuit of total war, while Italian Fascism was more pragmatic and accommodated existing power structures (the monarchy, the Catholic Church) to a greater degree.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The Lateran Pacts (1929) show Mussolini's accommodation of the Catholic Church, while Hitler's relationship with the churches was more antagonistic; (2) Nazi racial ideology (Nuremberg Laws, 1935) had no direct parallel in Italian Fascism until German pressure in the late 1930s; (3) both used state-controlled media and mass rallies (Piazza Venezia, Nuremberg) to build cult-of-personality regimes.
- **Best context starter:** The devastation and disillusionment following World War I created political vacuums across Europe that enabled authoritarian movements to seize power by promising national renewal, order, and opposition to both liberal democracy and Bolshevism.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the differences reflect the distinct national contexts: Germany's deeper industrialization, its Prussian military tradition, the specificity of the Versailles humiliation, and the more severe impact of the Depression all radicalized German politics beyond what Italian conditions produced.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating Fascism and Nazism as identical or as a single phenomenon without analyzing their distinct features and explaining why they differed.

### Comparison 7 (Unit 8)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare decolonization in British India (1947) and French Algeria (1954-1962).

- **Best thesis direction:** Both were driven by nationalist movements and the weakening of European empires after WWII, but British decolonization in India was a negotiated (though violent) transfer of power to established political organizations, while French decolonization in Algeria was a prolonged, brutal war that threatened French domestic politics because Algeria was legally considered part of France, not merely a colony.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Geography
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The Indian National Congress provided a unified political vehicle for negotiating independence, while Algerian nationalism was led through armed insurgency (FLN); (2) over one million European settlers (pieds-noirs) in Algeria complicated French withdrawal in ways that had no parallel in India; (3) the Algerian War caused the collapse of the French Fourth Republic and de Gaulle's return to power (1958).
- **Best context starter:** World War II fatally weakened the legitimacy and material capacity of European colonial empires, as wartime rhetoric about freedom and self-determination contradicted the continuation of colonial rule.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the key variable was the legal and demographic relationship between colony and metropole: Algeria's status as a department of France (with a large settler population) made decolonization a domestic political crisis, while India's status as a separate colony (with a tiny British administrative class) made withdrawal politically feasible.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Narrating each case separately without drawing explicit comparative conclusions about what factors explain the different decolonization processes.

### Comparison 8 (Units 8-9)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Compare the challenges to Soviet authority in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968).

- **Best thesis direction:** Both were reform movements within the Soviet bloc that sought to liberalize communist rule and were crushed by Soviet military intervention, but Hungary's uprising was more spontaneous and violent while Czechoslovakia's Prague Spring was a top-down reform led by party leaders -- and the international responses differed, with 1968 producing the Brezhnev Doctrine that formalized the Soviet right to intervene.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Intellectual, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Hungary's revolution began with student protests and escalated to armed resistance when PM Imre Nagy announced withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact; (2) Czechoslovakia's reforms under Dubcek ("socialism with a human face") included press freedom and decentralization but did not attempt to leave the Warsaw Pact; (3) the Brezhnev Doctrine (1968) explicitly asserted the Soviet Union's right to intervene in any socialist state, codifying the lesson of both interventions.
- **Best context starter:** After Stalin's death in 1953, Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalinist excesses at the Twentieth Party Congress (1956) raised expectations for reform across the Soviet bloc.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the different characters of the two movements reflected the different political cultures of Hungary (which had a stronger liberal tradition and a more traumatic Stalinist experience under Rakosi) and Czechoslovakia (where reform emerged from within the party itself), but that the Soviet response was nearly identical in both cases, suggesting that Moscow's red lines had not changed between 1956 and 1968.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Simply narrating the events of each uprising without comparatively analyzing the nature of the movements, the causes of Soviet intervention, and the differing consequences.

## Continuity and Change Over Time (CCOT) Prompts

### CCOT 1 (Units 1-2)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which European economic systems changed in the period 1450-1648.

- **Best thesis direction:** European economic systems changed dramatically through the rise of global commerce, the price revolution, and the emergence of joint-stock capitalism, but continuities persisted in the form of agrarian subsistence for the majority of the population, guild-based urban production, and landed aristocratic wealth as the primary marker of social status.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Technology, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the Columbian Exchange and Atlantic trade routes created a global commercial network centered on Europe; (2) Change: joint-stock companies (Dutch East India Company, 1602) introduced new forms of capital organization; (3) Continuity: the vast majority of Europeans remained subsistence agriculturalists throughout the period.
- **Best context starter:** In 1450, European economic life was overwhelmingly local and agrarian, organized around manorial agriculture, guild-regulated urban crafts, and Mediterranean-centered long-distance trade.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that change was geographically uneven: Atlantic-facing states experienced dramatic commercial transformation, while landlocked Central and Eastern European regions saw the reinforcement of traditional agrarian systems (second serfdom), demonstrating that "European" economic change was a regional, not universal, phenomenon.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing only changes without acknowledging significant continuities, or treating all of Europe as a uniform economic space.

### CCOT 2 (Unit 3)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the role of religion in European political life changed in the period 1517-1715.

- **Best thesis direction:** Religion's role changed from a unifying framework for political legitimacy (medieval Christendom) to a source of devastating conflict (Wars of Religion) and ultimately to a subordinated tool of state power (cuius regio, eius religio, revocation of the Edict of Nantes), but the continuity was that no European state became truly secular -- religion remained essential to political legitimacy throughout the period.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Religious, Political, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the Peace of Augsburg (1555) and Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the principle that rulers determined their state's religion, subordinating faith to political authority; (2) Change: political theorists like Bodin and Hobbes developed secular justifications for sovereignty; (3) Continuity: Louis XIV's revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) demonstrated that religious uniformity was still seen as essential to political unity.
- **Best context starter:** In 1500, the Catholic Church was the only Christian institution in Western Europe and exercised enormous political influence through papal authority, church courts, and clerical control of education and social welfare.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the apparent secularization of political life was actually a change in who controlled religion rather than a diminishment of religion's political role -- states co-opted religious authority rather than eliminating it.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Narrating a simple linear trajectory from "religious politics" to "secular politics" without acknowledging the persistence of religious authority in political life.

---

### CCOT 3 (Units 4-5)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which conceptions of individual rights changed in Europe in the period 1648-1815.

- **Best thesis direction:** Conceptions of individual rights changed profoundly through Enlightenment philosophy (natural rights, social contract theory) and revolutionary practice (declarations of rights in America and France), but continuities persisted in the exclusion of women, non-Europeans, the propertyless, and religious minorities from full rights -- the universalist language of rights masked persistent particularism.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Intellectual, Political, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689) grounded rights in natural law rather than divine grant or custom; (2) Change: the Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789) asserted universal natural rights; (3) Continuity: Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) and her subsequent execution demonstrated the limits of revolutionary universalism.
- **Best context starter:** Before the Enlightenment, European political thought generally understood rights as particular privileges granted by custom, charter, or royal decree to specific groups (nobles, guilds, towns) rather than as universal attributes of individuals.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the Napoleonic Code (1804) embodied both change and continuity: it codified legal equality for men and abolished feudal privileges (change) but also restricted women's property rights and reinstated slavery in French colonies (continuity), showing that the revolutionary expansion of rights was selective and contested.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Presenting a triumphalist narrative of steadily expanding rights without grappling with the exclusions and regressions within the period.

### CCOT 4 (Unit 5)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the social structure of France changed as a result of the Revolution and Napoleonic period (1789-1815).

- **Best thesis direction:** The Revolution and Napoleonic era produced significant changes -- the abolition of feudal privileges, legal equality under the Napoleonic Code, the destruction of the old aristocratic monopoly on office, and the rise of a bourgeois and meritocratic elite -- but continuities persisted in the form of economic inequality, the predominance of rural peasant life, and the eventual partial restoration of aristocratic influence.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Social, Political, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the August 4 Decrees (1789) abolished feudal dues and privileges of the First and Second Estates; (2) Change: Napoleon's Legion of Honor and career-open-to-talent principle replaced birth-based status with meritocratic advancement; (3) Continuity: the Restoration (1814-1815) returned the Bourbon monarchy and partially restored aristocratic land and titles.
- **Best context starter:** Pre-revolutionary France was organized into three legally defined estates -- the clergy, the nobility, and everyone else -- with the first two estates enjoying tax exemptions, legal privileges, and monopoly access to the highest positions in the state, military, and Church.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the most durable change was not in who held power but in the legal framework that defined power: even when aristocrats returned after 1815, they operated within a post-feudal legal system (the Napoleonic Code) that permanently eliminated the legal basis of the Old Regime's social hierarchy.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating the Revolution as a complete social transformation without acknowledging significant continuities in economic inequality and rural life.

### CCOT 5 (Unit 6)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the role of women in European society changed in the period 1750-1900.

- **Best thesis direction:** The period saw both expansion and restriction of women's roles: Enlightenment ideals and industrialization created new opportunities (education, factory work, reform movements) but also hardened gender ideology (the "cult of domesticity" or "separate spheres") that confined middle-class women to the home, while working-class women's labor expanded but was devalued.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Social, Intellectual, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) argued for women's education and rational equality; (2) Continuity/Change: industrialization drew women into factory work but at lower wages and under worse conditions than men; (3) Continuity: the Napoleonic Code (1804) and Victorian domestic ideology reinforced patriarchal legal authority and the ideal of female domesticity.
- **Best context starter:** In pre-industrial Europe, women participated in household economic production (spinning, dairying, market gardening) as part of the family economy, and their roles, while subordinate, were economically integrated with men's labor.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that industrialization's effects on women were class-dependent: working-class women entered factories and experienced economic exploitation, while middle-class women were increasingly defined by domestic ideology -- "change" and "continuity" depended on which women you examine.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing only middle-class women's experiences or only working-class women's experiences without acknowledging that class mediated the impact of broader trends.

### CCOT 6 (Units 7-8)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which European political boundaries and state structures changed in the period 1914-1950.

- **Best thesis direction:** The period saw the most dramatic redrawing of European political boundaries in modern history -- the collapse of four empires in WWI, the creation of new nation-states, the fascist and communist reconfigurations of the interwar period, and the post-WWII division into American and Soviet spheres -- but the continuity was the persistence of the nation-state as the basic unit of European politics, even as its sovereignty was increasingly constrained by superpower dominance.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Geography, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the Treaty of Versailles and associated treaties created Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, and the Baltic states from the ruins of empires; (2) Change: Nazi Germany's conquest and the Holocaust redrew boundaries through force and annihilation; (3) Change/Continuity: the post-WWII settlement divided Germany and Europe into two blocs but preserved the nation-state framework within each bloc.
- **Best context starter:** In 1914, much of Central and Eastern Europe was governed by multinational empires -- the Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman, Russian, and German empires -- whose boundaries had been relatively stable since the Congress of Vienna (1815).
- **Complexity move:** Note the paradox that the nation-state principle, which was supposed to bring stability by matching political boundaries to ethnic groups (Wilsonian self-determination), actually created new instabilities because ethnic populations rarely aligned with neat borders, producing minority problems that became pretexts for aggression.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Simply cataloging boundary changes without analyzing the principles, forces, and consequences driving those changes.

### CCOT 7 (Unit 9)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which European identity and integration changed in the period 1945-2000.

- **Best thesis direction:** European identity shifted from primarily national to partially supranational through the development of European institutions (ECSC, EEC, EU), the creation of common markets, and eventually common citizenship, but national identities remained the primary form of political allegiance for most Europeans, and integration repeatedly encountered resistance rooted in national sovereignty concerns.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Economic, Social
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the Schuman Plan (1950) and Treaty of Rome (1957) created supranational economic institutions; (2) Change: the Maastricht Treaty (1992) established EU citizenship and laid groundwork for the euro; (3) Continuity: resistance to European federalism (de Gaulle's "Europe of nations," British Euroscepticism, Danish and French referendum rejections of EU treaties) demonstrated the persistence of national identity.
- **Best context starter:** In 1945, Europe lay in ruins, divided by the devastation of two world wars and the emerging Cold War, and the idea of a unified Europe seemed utopian given centuries of nationalist conflict.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that European integration itself changed character over the period: from an elite-driven, technocratic project focused on preventing war (1950s-1970s) to a broader political project that raised democratic legitimacy questions as it expanded into areas (currency, borders, legal systems) that directly affected citizens' daily lives, generating both deeper integration and deeper resistance.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Presenting European integration as a smooth, inevitable process without acknowledging resistance, setbacks, and the ongoing tension between national and supranational identity.

### CCOT 8 (Units 1-9 Synthesis)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the relationship between European states and their populations changed in the period 1648-1914.

- **Best thesis direction:** The state-population relationship transformed from absolutist monarchies governing passive subjects to nation-states claiming to represent active citizens, driven by Enlightenment thought, revolutionary upheaval, and the demands of industrial society -- but continuities persisted in the form of elite domination, limited suffrage, and the use of state power to suppress dissent.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Change: the French Revolution replaced the doctrine of divine-right monarchy with popular sovereignty; (2) Change: the expansion of suffrage across the 19th century (Reform Acts in Britain, universal male suffrage in France by 1848); (3) Continuity: Bismarck's Germany combined universal male suffrage with authoritarian chancellorial government, demonstrating that expanded participation did not necessarily mean democratic governance.
- **Best context starter:** The Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the modern state system in which sovereign rulers exercised supreme authority within defined territorial boundaries, but the question of the ruler's relationship to the ruled remained contested for the next three centuries.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the state's capacity to affect its population's lives increased continuously (through taxation, conscription, education, and regulation) regardless of whether governance was authoritarian or democratic -- the growth of state power was the deeper continuity underlying the more visible changes in political ideology.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Narrating a simple progress narrative from absolutism to democracy without acknowledging the persistence of elite power and authoritarian governance throughout the period.

### DBQ 1 (Units 1-2)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which economic motivations drove European exploration and colonization in the period 1450-1600.

**Possible document types:**
- A letter from a Portuguese merchant describing profits from the spice trade
- An excerpt from a papal bull (Inter Caetera, 1493) granting Spain authority over newly "discovered" lands
- A Spanish missionary's account of converting indigenous peoples
- A map showing Atlantic trade routes and wind patterns
- An excerpt from a charter establishing a joint-stock trading company
- A journal entry from Columbus describing gold and resources in the Caribbean
- A critical account from Bartolome de Las Casas describing exploitation of indigenous labor

- **Best thesis direction:** Economic motivations were the primary driver of European exploration and colonization, but they were consistently intertwined with religious and political motivations, making it impossible to isolate economic factors as the sole cause.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Religious, Geography, Political
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Columbus's journal entries explicitly reference gold and commercial opportunities; (2) the joint-stock company charter shows the institutional infrastructure built around profit extraction; (3) the papal bull demonstrates that even religious authorization served the economic and political interests of Iberian monarchies.
- **Best context starter:** By the mid-fifteenth century, European demand for Asian luxury goods -- spices, silk, and precious metals -- was constrained by the expense and insecurity of overland trade routes through the Middle East, controlled by Ottoman and Venetian intermediaries.
- **Complexity move:** Use the Las Casas document to show that the relationship between economic and religious motivations was not harmonious but deeply contested -- some Europeans recognized that economic exploitation contradicted the Christian mission they claimed to be pursuing.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Simply sorting documents into "economic" and "non-economic" piles without analyzing how economic motivations intersected with and were reinforced by religious and political ones.

### DBQ 2 (Units 3-4)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which absolutist monarchs successfully controlled the nobility in the period 1648-1740.

**Possible document types:**
- An excerpt from Louis XIV's memoirs on managing the court at Versailles
- A letter from a French noble complaining about required attendance at court
- A description of the Table of Ranks established by Peter the Great (1722)
- An account of the Fronde rebellions in France (1648-1653)
- A Prussian noble's petition to Frederick William I regarding military service obligations
- A description of court ritual and etiquette at Versailles
- An excerpt from a political theorist (Bossuet) on divine-right monarchy

- **Best thesis direction:** Absolutist monarchs achieved significant but incomplete control over the nobility through a combination of patronage, cultural coercion, and institutional reform, but noble resistance and the monarchs' dependence on noble cooperation for local governance and military service limited the extent of royal dominance.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Economic
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) Louis XIV's use of Versailles as a mechanism of noble surveillance and competition for royal favor; (2) Peter the Great's Table of Ranks tying noble status to state service rather than birth; (3) the Fronde's legacy demonstrating both the threat of noble rebellion and the monarchy's eventual triumph over it.
- **Best context starter:** The relationship between European monarchs and their nobility had been the central tension of medieval and early modern politics, as kings sought to consolidate power while nobles defended their hereditary privileges and local autonomy.
- **Complexity move:** Compare the different strategies: Louis XIV used cultural seduction (Versailles), Peter the Great used institutional coercion (Table of Ranks), and Frederick William I used militarization -- the variety of approaches suggests there was no single model of absolutist control, and each carried different costs and limitations.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Describing only the monarchs' strategies without acknowledging evidence of noble resistance, compromise, or the limits of royal control.

### DBQ 3 (Unit 5)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the ideals of the French Revolution were realized in the period 1789-1799.

**Possible document types:**
- The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (1789)
- A speech by Robespierre defending the Terror as necessary to preserve the Republic
- A petition from a women's political club demanding inclusion in revolutionary citizenship
- An excerpt from the Law of Suspects (1793) authorizing arrest of suspected counter-revolutionaries
- A peasant's account of the abolition of feudal dues
- The Constitution of 1795 (Directory) limiting suffrage to property owners
- A sans-culotte pamphlet demanding economic equality and price controls

- **Best thesis direction:** The Revolution realized its ideals of legal equality and the destruction of feudal privilege in significant ways, but the ideals of liberty and universal rights were repeatedly compromised by the demands of war, internal conflict, and the exclusion of women and the poor from full citizenship.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Political, Social, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The abolition of feudal dues directly realized the Revolution's promise of legal equality; (2) the Law of Suspects and the Terror contradicted the Revolution's commitment to individual liberty; (3) the Directory's property-based suffrage showed the Revolution's retreat from universal democratic ideals.
- **Best context starter:** The French Revolution began in 1789 with a fiscal crisis that forced Louis XVI to convene the Estates-General, but it quickly became a fundamental challenge to the entire social and political order of the Old Regime.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that the Revolution's failure to fully realize its ideals was not simply a betrayal but reflected genuine tensions within the ideals themselves -- between liberty and equality, between individual rights and collective security, between universal principles and the practical demands of governing a society in crisis.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Either uncritically celebrating the Revolution as a triumph of liberty or simply condemning the Terror as a failure, without grappling with the complexity of how ideals interact with political reality.

### DBQ 4 (Unit 6)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which industrialization changed family structures and gender roles in Europe in the period 1750-1900.

**Possible document types:**
- An excerpt from a factory inspector's report on women and children in textile mills
- A middle-class advice manual prescribing separate spheres for men and women
- Testimony from a child laborer before a parliamentary commission
- A statistical table showing women's workforce participation rates
- An excerpt from Friedrich Engels's Condition of the Working Class in England
- A letter from a middle-class woman describing her domestic responsibilities
- A working-class family's household budget showing multiple wage earners

- **Best thesis direction:** Industrialization fundamentally altered family structures by separating the workplace from the home, creating class-differentiated gender roles: the middle-class "cult of domesticity" idealized women's exclusion from paid labor, while working-class families required women's and children's wage work for survival, meaning that "change" in gender roles depended heavily on class position.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Social, Economic, Intellectual
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The factory inspector's report shows women's active participation in industrial labor, challenging the domesticity ideal; (2) the middle-class advice manual shows the prescriptive ideology of separate spheres; (3) the working-class budget shows that the reality of working-class family life contradicted middle-class ideals.
- **Best context starter:** Before industrialization, most European families functioned as integrated economic units in which all members -- men, women, and children -- contributed to household production, whether in agriculture, artisan crafts, or domestic industry.
- **Complexity move:** Use the tension between the prescriptive documents (advice manuals) and the descriptive documents (factory reports, budgets) to argue that the "separate spheres" ideology was not a description of reality but an aspiration of the middle class that was imposed as a norm on society, masking the continued economic participation of most women.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating all women as a single category and ignoring the class differences that produced fundamentally different experiences of industrialization.

### DBQ 5 (Units 7-8)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which the experience of total war in the period 1914-1945 transformed European societies.

**Possible document types:**
- A government propaganda poster urging women to work in munitions factories (WWI)
- An excerpt from the Beveridge Report (1942) proposing a welfare state in Britain
- A soldier's letter describing trench warfare conditions
- A photograph of a destroyed European city (1945)
- An excerpt from a speech by a fascist leader invoking wartime sacrifice to justify authoritarian rule
- Statistical data on European civilian and military casualties, 1914-1945
- A memoir excerpt from a Holocaust survivor

- **Best thesis direction:** Total war transformed European societies by destroying the pre-1914 social order, expanding state power into every aspect of life, mobilizing entire populations (including women and colonial subjects) for war production, and ultimately discrediting the nationalist ideologies that had fueled the wars -- but the transformation was uneven and some pre-war structures (class hierarchies, colonial empires) survived longer than others.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Social, Political, Economic, Technology
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The propaganda poster shows the wartime expansion of women's economic roles; (2) the Beveridge Report demonstrates how wartime mobilization led to the welfare state; (3) the casualty statistics quantify the scale of destruction that made the old order unsustainable.
- **Best context starter:** Before 1914, European civilization was characterized by confidence in progress, the stability of empires, and a social order in which class, gender, and colonial hierarchies were considered natural and permanent.
- **Complexity move:** Argue that total war produced contradictory transformations: it simultaneously empowered democratic forces (welfare states, women's suffrage, decolonization movements) and authoritarian ones (fascism, Stalinism), suggesting that the destruction of the old order did not determine what would replace it.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Listing wartime changes without analyzing how the specific mechanisms of total war (mass mobilization, industrial warfare, home front targeting) produced those changes.

### DBQ 6 (Unit 9)

**PRACTICE PROMPT:** Evaluate the extent to which globalization changed European economies and cultures in the period 1970-2000.

**Possible document types:**
- A graph showing the growth of intra-European trade after the Single European Act (1986)
- An excerpt from a speech by Margaret Thatcher defending free-market deregulation
- A French farmer's protest statement against American cultural imports and EU agricultural policy
- A photograph of a McDonald's restaurant in a European city center
- An excerpt from a European Commission report on the benefits of the single market
- A newspaper article about anti-globalization protests in Genoa (2001)
- Statistical data on immigration to Western Europe from former colonies and Eastern Europe

- **Best thesis direction:** Globalization transformed European economies through market integration, deregulation, and the expansion of trade, while also provoking cultural resistance from those who saw it as threatening national identity, local traditions, and social protections -- the tension between economic integration and cultural preservation became a defining feature of late twentieth-century European politics.
- **Strongest Geo-SPRITE lenses:** Economic, Social, Political
- **Best 3 evidence items:** (1) The Single European Act and trade statistics show the measurable impact of economic integration; (2) the French farmer's protest demonstrates grassroots resistance to globalization's cultural and economic effects; (3) Thatcher's speech represents the ideological framework (neoliberalism) driving globalization policy.
- **Best context starter:** After the economic crises of the 1970s -- oil shocks, stagflation, and the breakdown of the Bretton Woods system -- European governments increasingly turned toward market liberalization and international economic integration as solutions to slow growth.
- **Complexity move:** Use the anti-globalization protest documents alongside the pro-integration documents to argue that globalization was not a single, coherent phenomenon but a contested process in which economic elites and ordinary citizens often had fundamentally different experiences and perspectives.
- **Common weak answer to avoid:** Treating globalization as either entirely positive (modernization) or entirely negative (cultural imperialism) without acknowledging that it produced both benefits and costs distributed unevenly across European societies.
