# Pick the Prompt with the Best Geo-SPRITE Coverage

The Long Essay Question gives you a choice among three prompts. You have fifteen minutes to plan and forty minutes to write. Choosing the right prompt is the most consequential decision you make, and Geo-SPRITE gives you a systematic method for making it. This toolkit walks through prompt diagnosis, thesis construction, paragraph organization, evidence selection, and complexity strategy.

---

## Prompt Diagnosis Method

You will see three LEQ prompts, each targeting a different time period but sharing the same historical thinking skill (causation, comparison, or continuity and change over time). Before you commit to a prompt, run every option through this five-question checklist.

### The Five-Question Checklist

**Question 1: Can I name at least three specific pieces of evidence?**

Not vague references. Specific events, documents, people, policies, treaties, or movements. If you cannot name three within thirty seconds, move to the next prompt.

Test yourself: For a prompt about the causes of World War I, you might list the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the alliance system (Triple Alliance and Triple Entente), and the naval arms race between Britain and Germany. Those are specific. "Imperialism was a cause" is a category, not evidence. You need the Berlin Conference, the Moroccan Crises, or the Boer War to make imperialism concrete.

**Question 2: Can I explain the causes, effects, comparisons, or changes the prompt is asking about?**

Identify the historical thinking skill. If it is causation, can you explain why the development happened and rank the causes? If it is comparison, can you identify meaningful similarities and differences rather than surface-level parallels? If it is continuity and change, can you identify what actually changed, what persisted, and why?

Test yourself: For a comparison prompt about the French and Russian Revolutions, can you go beyond "both overthrew monarchies" to explain how the French Revolution's bourgeois liberal phase differed structurally from the Bolshevik seizure of power?

**Question 3: Can I add a second Geo-SPRITE lens for complexity?**

Your main argument will operate through one or two lenses. Complexity requires bringing in an additional perspective. If your argument about industrialization focuses on Economic and Technological factors, can you add a Social lens (impact on family structure, gender roles, class formation) for complexity?

Test yourself: If you choose a prompt about the spread of nationalism in the nineteenth century and plan to argue through a Political lens, do you have enough knowledge to bring in an Intellectual lens (Herder, Fichte, romantic nationalism) or a Social lens (the role of mass education and literacy in constructing national identity)?

**Question 4: Can I avoid factual errors?**

This is the negative filter. If a prompt asks about a topic you know imprecisely, you risk placing events in the wrong century, confusing similar figures, or misattributing treaties. Factual errors undermine your credibility and can cost you evidence points.

Test yourself: If you are unsure whether the Congress of Vienna was 1815 or 1825, or whether the Edict of Nantes was issued by Henry IV or Louis XIV, the prompt that requires that knowledge is a risky choice.

**Question 5: Can I contextualize broadly?**

The rubric awards a point for contextualization: placing the topic within a broader historical context. This requires knowledge beyond the immediate topic. Can you connect the prompt's subject to larger developments, trends, or events in the same period?

Test yourself: For a prompt about the Protestant Reformation, can you connect it to the broader context of Renaissance humanism, the printing press, late-medieval anticlericalism, and the political fragmentation of the Holy Roman Empire?

**Scoring your options:** Run all three prompts through the checklist. The prompt where you answer "yes" to all five questions most confidently is your best choice. If two prompts tie, choose the one where your evidence is most specific, because specificity is what separates a 4 from a 6.

## 50 Thesis Templates

A strong thesis makes a historically defensible claim that responds to the prompt and establishes a line of reasoning. The templates below use real AP European History content. Adapt the structure to your specific prompt.

### Causation Thesis Templates

**1.** Although the immediate trigger of the French Revolution was the fiscal crisis of 1789, the deeper cause was the structural contradiction between an absolutist monarchy and an increasingly assertive bourgeoisie demanding political representation proportional to its economic power.

**2.** Although dynastic competition contributed to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War, the more fundamental cause was the failure of the Peace of Augsburg to accommodate Calvinism, which left the religious settlement of the Holy Roman Empire inherently unstable.

**3.** Although Enlightenment philosophy provided the intellectual justification for the abolition of serfdom in Russia, the primary cause of Alexander II's Emancipation Edict of 1861 was the military humiliation of the Crimean War, which exposed serfdom as an obstacle to modernization.

**4.** Although nationalist ideology motivated Italian unification, the process was driven more decisively by Piedmont-Sardinia's diplomatic manipulation under Cavour, particularly his alliance with Napoleon III against Austria.

**5.** Although Luther's theological challenge initiated the Protestant Reformation, the movement's rapid spread was caused primarily by the printing press, which allowed reformist ideas to circulate beyond the control of ecclesiastical censors.

**6.** Although militarism and the alliance system created the conditions for World War I, the immediate cause was the failure of diplomacy during the July Crisis of 1914, when rigid mobilization timetables overrode political decision-making.

**7.** Although the Industrial Revolution originated in Britain due to geographic advantages such as coal deposits and navigable waterways, its sustained growth depended on political factors including parliamentary stability, property rights, and a legal framework that encouraged capital investment.

**8.** Although colonial competition heightened tensions among European powers in the late nineteenth century, the Scramble for Africa was caused primarily by the economic logic of industrial capitalism, which demanded new markets and raw materials.

**9.** Although the Congress of Vienna restored conservative monarchies across Europe, the persistence of liberal and nationalist movements after 1815 was caused by the irreversible social changes of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic era, including the abolition of feudal privileges and the spread of legal equality.

**10.** Although the immediate trigger of the Russian Revolution of 1917 was wartime hardship, the deeper cause was the tsarist regime's failure to implement meaningful political reform after 1905, leaving no institutional channel for popular grievances.

**11.** Although technological innovation drove the second Industrial Revolution, the primary cause of rapid industrialization in Germany after 1871 was state policy, including tariff protection, investment in railroads, and the creation of a unified national market through the Zollverein.

**12.** Although ideological opposition to communism motivated Western containment policy, the Cold War division of Europe was caused primarily by the security dilemma created by the power vacuum in Central Europe after 1945.

**13.** Although religious conviction motivated individual Crusaders, the Crusades were caused primarily by papal ambition to assert leadership over European Christendom and channel feudal violence outward.

**14.** Although the Great Depression originated in the American financial system, its devastating impact on Europe was caused by the structural weaknesses of the post-Versailles economic order, particularly war debts, reparations, and dependence on American capital flows.

**15.** Although the witch trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries reflected genuine theological belief in diabolism, their intensification was caused by the social disruptions of the Reformation, the Little Ice Age, and the breakdown of communal structures in an era of confessional conflict.

**16.** Although the philosophes critiqued the Old Regime's irrationality, the primary cause of the French Revolution's radicalization was the political failure of the moderate constitutional monarchy, which lost legitimacy through Louis XVI's attempted flight to Varennes and his perceived collaboration with foreign powers.

**17.** Although the Black Death of 1347-1351 caused catastrophic demographic collapse, its long-term effect was to improve the bargaining power of surviving peasants, contributing to the decline of serfdom in Western Europe.

### Comparison Thesis Templates

**18.** Although the French Revolution and the Haitian Revolution both invoked Enlightenment principles of liberty and equality, they differed fundamentally because the Haitian Revolution challenged the racial hierarchy that the French Revolution largely preserved.

**19.** Although both the British and French models of industrialization relied on textile production as a leading sector, they differed in pace and state involvement because Britain industrialized through private enterprise and market forces, while French industrialization depended more heavily on state investment and protective tariffs.

**20.** Although both the Protestant Reformation and the Catholic Counter-Reformation sought to purify Christian practice, they differed in their relationship to political authority because the Reformation fractured political-religious unity, while the Counter-Reformation reinforced it through the alliance of the papacy with Catholic monarchs.

**21.** Although both Spain and England built Atlantic empires in the sixteenth century, their colonial models differed because Spain pursued direct territorial conquest and resource extraction through the encomienda system, while England initially relied on joint-stock companies and settler colonies oriented toward agricultural production.

**22.** Although both Napoleon and Hitler sought to dominate continental Europe, their imperial projects differed fundamentally because Napoleon spread Enlightenment legal reforms and administrative modernization, while Hitler pursued racial extermination and the destruction of the European state system.

**23.** Although both Russia and the Ottoman Empire undertook modernization programs in the nineteenth century, Russia's reforms under Alexander II were more structurally transformative because they addressed serfdom and created limited local self-government through the zemstvos, while Ottoman Tanzimat reforms focused on administrative centralization without dismantling the millet system.

**24.** Although both the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the French Revolution of 1789 challenged absolute monarchy, they differed in outcome because the Glorious Revolution produced a stable constitutional settlement through elite negotiation, while the French Revolution radicalized through popular mobilization and produced a decade of political instability.

**25.** Although both Italian and German unification were driven by nationalist ideology and accomplished through war, they differed in political structure because Italian unification produced a weak liberal parliamentary state, while German unification under Bismarck created an authoritarian federal empire dominated by Prussia.

**26.** Although both the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment challenged traditional authority, they differed in scope because the Scientific Revolution was confined to natural philosophy and a narrow intellectual elite, while the Enlightenment extended critical reason to politics, religion, and social organization.

**27.** Although both Tudor England and Bourbon France built centralized monarchies in the early modern period, they differed in their relationship to representative institutions because the Tudors governed through Parliament, which preserved a tradition of consent, while the Bourbons marginalized the Estates-General after 1614.

**28.** Although both the Marshall Plan and Soviet economic aid sought to rebuild postwar Europe, they differed in method and outcome because the Marshall Plan promoted market-oriented recovery and transatlantic integration, while Soviet aid imposed centrally planned economies and political subordination on Eastern Europe.

**29.** Although both the Renaissance in Italy and the Northern Renaissance emphasized classical learning, they differed in focus because the Italian Renaissance prioritized secular humanism and artistic patronage, while the Northern Renaissance applied humanist methods to religious reform and vernacular literature.

**30.** Although both fascist Italy and Nazi Germany rejected liberal democracy, they differed in the degree of totalitarian control because Mussolini's regime accommodated the monarchy, the Catholic Church, and traditional elites, while Hitler's regime subordinated all institutions to the party and the Fuhrer principle.

**31.** Although both the Austrian and Ottoman Empires were multinational states that collapsed after World War I, they differed in the nature of their dissolution because the Austrian Empire fragmented along ethno-national lines already sharpened by nineteenth-century nationalist movements, while the Ottoman dissolution was shaped more by great-power partition and the emergence of Kemalist Turkish nationalism.

**32.** Although both Calvinist Geneva and Jesuit missions in the Americas sought to create model Christian communities, they differed because Calvin imposed discipline on an existing European urban population through consistory courts, while Jesuits adapted Christianity to indigenous cultures through accommodation strategies.

**33.** Although both mercantilism and free-trade liberalism sought to maximize national wealth, they differed fundamentally in their assumptions because mercantilism treated trade as a zero-sum competition requiring state regulation, while free-trade liberalism assumed that unregulated exchange benefited all parties.

### Continuity and Change over Time Thesis Templates

**34.** From 1450 to 1648, European religious life changed dramatically as the Protestant Reformation shattered the unity of Western Christendom, but the centrality of religion to political legitimacy persisted, as both Catholic and Protestant rulers continued to invoke divine authority to justify their power.

**35.** From 1789 to 1871, European political structures changed as revolutionary and nationalist movements dismantled the Old Regime's dynastic order, but the dominance of elite governance persisted because the new nation-states were built by conservative statesmen like Bismarck and Cavour who channeled nationalism to serve existing power structures.

**36.** From 1750 to 1900, the European economy changed fundamentally as industrialization replaced agricultural production as the primary source of wealth, but economic inequality persisted and intensified as industrial capitalism created a new class of wage laborers who lacked the subsistence safety net of the pre-industrial peasantry.

**37.** From 1914 to 1945, European international relations changed as the balance-of-power system collapsed into two world wars, but the pattern of great-power competition for continental dominance persisted, shifting from Anglo-German rivalry to the Soviet-American confrontation that defined the postwar order.

**38.** From 1450 to 1750, European interactions with the wider world changed as maritime exploration created global trade networks and colonial empires, but European ethnocentrism and the economic exploitation of non-European peoples persisted, evolving from the encomienda system to plantation slavery to mercantilist extraction.

**39.** From 1815 to 1914, the social structure of Europe changed as industrialization and urbanization created a large urban working class and an influential bourgeoisie, but aristocratic dominance in politics and the military persisted in most states, particularly in Russia, Prussia, and Austria-Hungary, where landed elites retained disproportionate power.

**40.** From 1648 to 1815, the basis of political authority in Europe changed as Enlightenment ideas about popular sovereignty and natural rights challenged divine-right absolutism, but centralized executive power persisted, as even revolutionary regimes (the Committee of Public Safety, Napoleon) concentrated authority to manage crises.

**41.** From 1500 to 1700, European intellectual life changed as the Scientific Revolution replaced Aristotelian natural philosophy with empirical and mathematical methods, but the authority of religious institutions over intellectual life persisted in significant ways, as demonstrated by the Galileo affair and the continued integration of theology into university curricula.

**42.** From 1945 to 1989, the political map of Europe changed as the Cold War divided the continent into NATO and Warsaw Pact blocs, but the aspiration toward European integration persisted, manifesting in the European Coal and Steel Community, the Treaty of Rome, and the eventual reunification of Europe after the fall of the Berlin Wall.

**43.** From 1300 to 1500, European demographic and economic patterns changed dramatically as the Black Death reduced the population by a third and disrupted the manorial economy, but the hierarchical social structure of medieval Europe persisted because elites used legislation like the Statute of Laborers (1351) to suppress peasant mobility and preserve their economic privileges.

**44.** From 1789 to 1848, the role of women in European public life changed as revolutionary ideology raised questions about female citizenship and education, but patriarchal legal structures persisted as the Napoleonic Code and similar reforms codified male authority over property, marriage, and public participation.

**45.** From 1850 to 1914, European imperialism changed in character as the informal influence of the early nineteenth century gave way to the formal territorial partition of Africa and Asia, but the underlying economic motivation persisted because both phases were driven by the need for raw materials, markets, and capital investment opportunities generated by industrial capitalism.

**46.** From 1517 to 1648, the relationship between religion and the state in the Holy Roman Empire changed as the Reformation introduced confessional pluralism and triggered a century of religious warfare, but the principle that rulers determined the religion of their territories persisted from the Peace of Augsburg through the Peace of Westphalia.

**47.** From 1848 to 1914, the nature of European nationalism changed from the liberal, civic ideal of self-determination championed by Mazzini to an aggressive, exclusionary ethnic nationalism exemplified by pan-Germanism and anti-Semitic movements, but the core claim that the nation was the legitimate basis of political organization persisted across both phases.

**48.** From 1919 to 1939, European democratic governance changed as the Versailles settlement established parliamentary republics across Central and Eastern Europe, but the fragility of democratic institutions persisted as economic crisis, political polarization, and authoritarian movements overthrew democracies in Italy, Germany, Spain, and most of Eastern Europe.

**49.** From 1700 to 1800, the European economy changed as mercantilist state-directed trade gave way to early free-market theory articulated by the Physiocrats and Adam Smith, but state intervention in the economy persisted because even liberal governments maintained tariffs, navigation acts, and colonial monopolies when free trade threatened domestic interests.

**50.** From 1550 to 1700, European warfare changed as the Military Revolution introduced gunpowder technology, professional standing armies, and costly fortification systems, but the aristocratic monopoly on military leadership persisted because officer commissions remained tied to noble birth and patronage rather than merit.

## Paragraph Organization by Lens

Each body paragraph should be organized around a single Geo-SPRITE lens. This does not mean the paragraph discusses only one category. It means the paragraph's claim operates through one primary lens, and evidence within the paragraph supports that claim.

**Paragraph structure:**

1. **Topic sentence:** State the paragraph's claim through the lens. Example: "The most significant political cause of the French Revolution was the monarchy's loss of legitimacy after decades of fiscal mismanagement and the failure of reform."
2. **Evidence 1:** Provide a specific piece of evidence that supports the claim through this lens. Explain its significance. "Louis XVI's attempt to impose new taxes through the Assembly of Notables in 1787 revealed the crown's inability to reform itself, as the privileged orders refused to surrender their fiscal exemptions."
3. **Evidence 2:** Provide a second piece of evidence. "The convocation of the Estates-General in 1789 for the first time since 1614 demonstrated that the monarchy had exhausted its ability to govern without consent, effectively admitting that absolutism had failed."
4. **Analysis:** Connect the evidence back to the thesis. Explain why this lens matters more than (or interacts with) the alternative. "While economic hardship and Enlightenment ideas created the conditions for revolution, it was the political collapse of the absolutist system that made revolution a practical possibility rather than an intellectual abstraction."

**Choosing your paragraph lenses:** Your essay should have two to three body paragraphs, each organized around a different lens. Choose the lenses that best match your evidence. If your thesis argues that the Industrial Revolution was driven primarily by technological innovation and economic incentives, your first body paragraph should address Technology, your second should address Economics, and your complexity paragraph can introduce a Social lens to show how the transformation affected workers, families, and class formation.

## Evidence Selection by Lens

Strong evidence is specific, accurately dated, and clearly connected to the argument. Geo-SPRITE helps you select evidence by ensuring that each piece of evidence works through a defined lens.

**Method:** After choosing your thesis and paragraph lenses, brainstorm evidence for each lens. Then select the two to three strongest pieces for each paragraph.

| Lens | Strong evidence | Weak evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Political | The Tennis Court Oath (June 1789) demonstrated the Third Estate's claim to political sovereignty | "There was political unrest" |
| Economic | The Continental System (1806-1814) disrupted European trade patterns and provoked widespread smuggling | "The economy was bad" |
| Social | The Factory Act of 1833 prohibited employment of children under nine in textile mills | "Workers suffered" |
| Religious | The Act of Supremacy (1534) made Henry VIII head of the Church of England | "Religion was important" |
| Intellectual | Voltaire's Lettres philosophiques (1733) used England as a model to critique French absolutism | "Enlightenment thinkers had ideas" |
| Technology | The development of the Bessemer process (1856) reduced the cost of steel production by 80 percent | "Technology improved" |
| Geographic | Britain's island geography and naval superiority allowed it to avoid continental invasion during the Napoleonic Wars | "Geography mattered" |

**Evidence density rule:** Each body paragraph should contain at least two specific pieces of evidence. Specificity means names, dates, places, and consequences. "Napoleon" is not evidence. "Napoleon's Continental System, implemented in 1806, aimed to weaken Britain economically by prohibiting European trade with British ports" is evidence.

## Complexity Strategy: How to Add a Second Geo-SPRITE Category

The complexity point requires you to demonstrate a nuanced understanding that goes beyond your core argument. The most reliable method is to add a second Geo-SPRITE lens that qualifies, extends, or complicates your thesis.

### Five Approaches to Complexity

**Approach 1: The qualifying lens.** Your thesis argues that Political factors caused X. Your complexity move argues that Economic conditions were a necessary precondition that made Political change possible.

Example: "Although the political crisis of the Estates-General triggered the French Revolution, the monarchy would not have faced this crisis without the fiscal pressures created by French involvement in the American Revolution and decades of structural deficit spending. Political collapse required economic preconditions."

**Approach 2: The counterexample through a different lens.** Your thesis argues that Intellectual change drove the Scientific Revolution. Your complexity move identifies a case where Social factors (patronage, institutional access, gender exclusion) determined which intellectual developments succeeded and which were marginalized.

Example: "While the intellectual achievements of the Scientific Revolution depended on new methods of empirical inquiry, the social structures of patronage and academic exclusion shaped which discoveries gained recognition. Margaret Cavendish's contributions to natural philosophy were marginalized not because they lacked intellectual merit but because the Royal Society excluded women."

**Approach 3: The chronological shift in lens dominance.** Your thesis argues that one lens was primary during the first part of the period, but a different lens became primary later.

Example: "In the early phase of European exploration (1450-1550), Geographic factors such as Portugal's Atlantic location and advances in navigation technology were the primary drivers of expansion. By the seventeenth century, however, Economic factors, particularly the profitability of plantation agriculture and the transatlantic slave trade, had become the dominant motive for colonial enterprise."

**Approach 4: The regional variation through a different lens.** Your thesis argues that a development unfolded one way in Western Europe. Your complexity move shows that the same development unfolded differently in Eastern or Southern Europe because a different lens was dominant.

Example: "While the abolition of serfdom in Western Europe was driven by Economic factors, particularly the commercialization of agriculture and the growth of wage labor, the persistence of serfdom in Eastern Europe until the nineteenth century reflects the Political power of the landed nobility, who used institutions like the Prussian Junker class and the Russian mir to maintain coerced labor systems."

**Approach 5: The interaction of lenses.** Rather than arguing that one lens was more important, argue that two lenses reinforced each other in ways that neither could have produced alone.

Example: "The rapid spread of the Protestant Reformation cannot be attributed to Religious conviction or Political opportunism alone. The interaction of the two was decisive: rulers who adopted Protestantism gained both the theological legitimacy of reform and the material benefits of confiscating church lands, while reformers who secured princely protection gained the institutional support necessary to establish new churches. Neither factor alone would have produced the Reformation's geographic reach or institutional durability."

### Complexity Placement

Place your complexity move in one of two locations:

1. **A dedicated complexity paragraph** after your body paragraphs but before your conclusion. This is the safest approach because it clearly signals to the reader that you are adding nuance.
2. **Woven into your body paragraphs** as qualifying statements. This is more sophisticated but risks being missed by a hurried reader. If you choose this approach, make the qualification explicit: "However, this political explanation is incomplete without considering the economic pressures that..."

### What Complexity Is Not

Complexity is not a summary of your essay. It is not restating your thesis in different words. It is not listing additional Geo-SPRITE categories without analysis. It is not a vague statement like "There were many factors involved." Complexity requires a specific, substantiated claim that qualifies or extends your argument through a lens you have not yet fully developed.

The rubric describes complexity as demonstrating "a nuanced understanding of the historical development that is the focus of the prompt." Nuance means acknowledging that historical causation is multi-layered, that change and continuity coexist, that the same development can look different depending on the analytical lens applied to it. Geo-SPRITE gives you the vocabulary to articulate that nuance. The argument is yours to make.
