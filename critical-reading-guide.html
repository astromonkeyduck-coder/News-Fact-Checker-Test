How to read a news claim closely.

A story can contain accurate facts and still leave you with a mistaken impression. The problem may be a missing comparison, an unsupported explanation, or a headline that goes further than the evidence beneath it. Close reading begins by identifying the precise claim you are being asked to accept.

Find the statement that needs evidence

Consider this fictional headline: “New safety program cuts incidents by 40 percent.” It makes two claims. One concerns a numerical change; the other credits the program for causing it.

Suppose incidents fell from five to three. That is a 40 percent decline, involving two fewer incidents. A fall from 500 to 300 is also 40 percent, involving 200 fewer incidents. The percentage is meaningful only when you understand its starting point, the period measured, and what was counted.

The claim about the program’s effect requires further evidence. Incidents might also have changed because of seasonal patterns, different reporting practices, or another intervention. A decline after a program began establishes a sequence. It does not, by itself, establish the explanation.

Follow the attribution

“Officials said” should lead you to a named official, department, statement, or record. Ask what that source was in a position to know. Someone who witnessed an event may be able to describe what happened in front of them while knowing little about what caused it.

Then inspect apparently separate accounts. If three articles cite the same press release, they share a source. To find additional corroboration, look for another evidentiary basis, such as separate observations, records, or reporting. Attribution and identifying the origin of material are also central to the Associated Press’s published reporting practices.

Read the qualification with the claim

Small phrases can define the scope of a finding: “among respondents,” “during the first quarter,” “preliminary estimate,” or “according to the company.” Carry those qualifications into your understanding of the story.

For a survey, ask who participated and what question they answered. For a statistic, check whether the article compares totals or rates. More incidents in a growing population and a higher rate per resident are different claims.

Watch percentage points as well. A measure rising from 10 percent to 15 percent increases by five percentage points and by 50 percent relative to its original value. Those descriptions refer to the same change, but sound quite different without the starting figures.

Put the conclusion in your own words

Try writing one sentence that states only what the evidence establishes. In the fictional example, it might be: “Recorded incidents fell from five to three in the measured period; the information presented does not establish how much of the change resulted from the program.”

Then identify the next piece of information that would matter. A longer series of observations, a suitable comparison group, or an explanation of how the records were collected may help you judge the claim. The question should follow from the uncertainty you have identified.

On a Noteworthy article, apply the same scrutiny to the claim, source attribution, dates, and any correction note. Our work should be open to the questions this guide teaches you to ask.
