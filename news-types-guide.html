Recognizing news, analysis, and opinion.

The same event can produce a news report, an explanation, an argument, and a promotional statement. These forms ask the reader to make different judgments. Knowing which one you are reading helps you understand what the writer is trying to establish.

The examples below are fictional. Imagine a city council has voted to replace an aging bridge.

Reporting the decision

“The council voted 6–1 on Tuesday to approve the replacement” is a factual claim. A news report should establish the vote, identify the proposal, and explain what the decision authorizes. The meeting record and project documents give readers a way to examine those details.

Wording matters here. Approval of a plan, approval of funding, and the start of construction are different developments. A precise article explains which has occurred.

Explaining its significance

An analysis might examine how the replacement could affect traffic or access to businesses. That explanation needs evidence connecting the decision to its possible consequences, such as the closure schedule, available alternative routes, and the assumptions behind a traffic study.

An explainer may instead describe how the project is funded or why the current bridge needs replacement. It supplies background a reader needs to understand the story. Both forms require care in separating established facts from interpretation and forecasts.

Arguing for a choice

“The city should repair the existing bridge instead” is an opinion. It advances a preferred course of action. Evaluate the supporting facts, the alternatives considered, and the values behind the argument. An author may place particular weight on cost, access, preservation, or long-term capacity.

An opinion label makes the argument’s purpose clearer. Factual statements within it still need support. A mistaken cost estimate remains a mistake even when it appears in a persuasive essay.

Recognizing the source’s own account

A city announcement may emphasize the benefits of its proposal. A contractor may describe the project through its own experience or commercial interest. Those accounts can contain useful information, but their origin matters when assessing what they include and omit.

Sponsored content adds a commercial relationship that should be disclosed. It can resemble an article in layout and tone, so look for the sponsorship label and the organization paying for the placement.

Understanding an automated summary

Automation describes how an item was produced, rather than the subject it covers. An automated summary of the council’s announcement may repeat the announcement accurately, or it may introduce an error while shortening it. Check the original source before treating the summary as a complete account.

Attribution to an agency, a publication byline, and evidence of human review convey different information. A professional layout cannot establish any of them on its own.

When you finish reading, identify the central claim and the kind of support it needs: evidence that an event occurred, reasoning about its consequences, or an argument for what should happen next. A clear label helps orient you; the content still deserves examination.
